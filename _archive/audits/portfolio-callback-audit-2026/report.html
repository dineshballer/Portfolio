Why the visits aren't turning into callbacks, and what to change on the Loan Deboarding and About pages.

An honest read of the GA4 data, measured against what recruiters and design leaders say they want in 2026, plus a direct answer to the question you actually asked: does this read as AI generated?

Scope: loan-deboarding + about Data window: 14–21 Jun 2026 12 sources cited No em dashes (your rule)

The short version

The pages are well built. The problem is that nothing on them is easy to verify, the writing has a recognizable AI cadence, and people leave before they reach your proof.

Two separate things are failing. Distribution: only 12 people saw the site in a week, so "no callback" is partly a volume problem no page edit can fix. Conversion: the few who land give the homepage about 10 seconds and the flagship case study about 25 seconds, which means they skim two screens and go. In 2026 the review is faster and more skeptical than the portfolio was built for: homepages get 10 to 15 seconds, full reviews get 2 to 3 minutes, and roughly 78% of recruiters run AI assisted screening before a human opens it. The fix is to lead with verifiable, hard-to-fake evidence and cut the polished abstraction.

2What the bar is now (recruiters + Aneta + UX leads)

I read the current guidance so the recommendations are grounded, not vibes. The themes were strikingly consistent across a 74-recruiter survey, the Fountain Institute's June 2026 hiring breakdown, and Aneta Kmiecik's recent posts.

  • Shipped and traceable beats polished. The first thing a lead checks: "are these products live and traceable, and if live, are they scaling?" A beautiful redesign that moved no metric reads as a concept project. (Harry Enaholo, 150+ portfolios reviewed; Muz.li 2026)
  • Show the trade-off, not the process. "Seniority lives in the moment you chose between two competing goods, or traded scope against time." Process diagrams and double-diamond structures now read as a tell that you confused a framework with thinking. (Fountain Institute; Tom Scott's 8 red flags)
  • AI sameness is the actual tell. Aneta Kmiecik, on AI-misused portfolios: "Stories sound like an ideal double diamond process, and layouts are repetitive. Everything is produced, generated, and based on average data. When every portfolio looks the same after using AI, the AI becomes a sign of sameness rather than capability."
  • But hiding AI is also a red flag. "No AI signals" is on the 2026 red-flag list. Showing how you direct AI, with judgment, is now load-bearing. You already do this well; see section 4.
  • Depth in one problem space beats range. Generic competency lines ("led end-to-end design," "worked cross-functionally") put you in the wrong category. Your mortgage/lending niche is an asset; lean in.
  • Cut anything you can't defend under pressure, and anything not real. Three strong case studies beat seven. Curated, not comprehensive.

3Does it look AI generated? Yes, in three specific ways

You asked directly, so here is the direct answer. The work is real, but three signals make a skeptical 2026 reviewer suspect otherwise. Two are fixable in an afternoon. One is structural.

3a. The writing has a recognizable AI cadence

The most-flagged AI pattern right now is the negation/contrast construction ("not X, it's Y") and the rule-of-three. One or two reads as good writing. Stacked, they read as generated. Your pages stack them.

Also: the exact triad "catch errors at the door / exceptions a lane / one release record" appears in the hero, the snapshot, and the problem aside. A human editor varies it. The verbatim echo is what reads as templated. The "quieter Tuesday" motif also repeats three times across the two pages.

3b. The layout repeats the same module about eight times

This is Aneta's "repetitive layouts" point exactly. Nearly every section of the Loan case study is the same block: eyebrow label, a clever statement headline, a one-line aside, then a grid of three cards with emoji icons. The sameness itself signals generation, even though you hand-built it. Heavy emoji iconography (🧩 ⚠️ 📊 📩 🚨) reinforces the "AI deck" look.

3c. The credibility gap (this is the real problem, bigger than wording)

Put yourself in the reviewer's seat. The Loan case study is marked "In development." Every screen carries "Designs modified · NDA" and the note says the mockups were "reimagined with AI assistance." The metrics are "projected" (~6 hrs) or small and perfectly round (9/9, 100%, 3/3).

Stack those together and a 2026 reviewer sees: a not-yet-shipped project, shown entirely through AI-generated images, with projected and round numbers, written in AI cadence. Every single credibility signal is the one reviewers now discount. That is why it can read as AI generated. The work is real; none of it is verifiable from the outside, and verifiability is the 2026 trust test.

What is already right, keep it: the domain specificity when it shows up (Encompass, MERS, Purchase Advices, REL-2026-0044) is the best anti-AI material on the site. The "What didn't work / What I'd do differently" retrospective is exactly the intellectual honesty reviewers reward. The "I build with AI, and own the calls" card is the right 2026 instinct. You need more of these and less of the polished abstraction around them.

4Loan Deboarding: what to change
P0

Add at least one genuinely human, hard-to-fake artifact

Right now 100% of the visuals are AI-recreated and NDA-stamped, which is precisely what makes it look generated. Add a real photo of a whiteboard, a phone snap of an early paper sketch, a screenshot of your actual Figma version history, or the real (redacted) tagging board instead of a recreated one. This one move does more against "looks AI generated" than any rewrite.

Why: artifacts are evidence that the work happened. uxfol.io and the AI red-flag literature both rank "no supporting artifacts" as a top trust killer.

P0

Make one thing clickable and verifiable

Embed a live Figma prototype people can actually click, above the fold. The 25-second dwell says they never reach your six shipped screens. A prototype is the most engaging element in a case study and keeps reviewers on the page longer.

Why: "live and traceable" is the first thing leads check. Verifiability beats polish.

P1

Pull the trade-off up. It is your strongest senior signal and it's buried.

Your retrospective already contains the best sentence in the whole case study: shipping bulk PA upload as a hard dependency that phase two now has to unwind, and "I'd hold it behind a feature flag from week one." That is seniority. Move it into a dedicated block near the top, titled something plain like "The call I'd defend."

Why: "seniority lives where you chose between two competing goods or traded scope against time" (Fountain Institute).

P1

Cut the length and kill the module repetition

Collapse ten near-identical sections to roughly five: Snapshot, Problem (one screen), two hero decisions with their trade-offs, Validation, Retrospective. Vary the layout between them so it stops reading as a template.

Why: repetitive layouts read as AI; recruiters skim in minutes and reward "removed everything that doesn't support the core narrative."

P2

Re-baseline the round metrics

"9/9," "100%," "3/3," "~6 hrs projected" read as inflated. Either give the method and baseline ("9 walkthroughs, ~45 min each, across 3 client orgs; 6 hrs estimated against measured follow-up + reconciliation time") or swap to numbers that are real and specific. A reviewer trusts "6 of 9" more than "100%."

Why: perfect numbers without a baseline trigger skepticism; traceable numbers anchor the story.

5About: what to change

This is your stickiest page, and it is beautifully made. The issue is the ratio: lots of belief (four principles, five manifesto cards, stacked aphorisms), little concrete proof. It sells temperament when it could sell evidence.

P0

Rewrite the experience bullets. They are textbook boilerplate.

These are exactly the generic competency lines the 2026 guidance says to cut. Each role should be one specific, defensible sentence with a noun only you could write: a product name, a real constraint, a real number.

Now (Dell)

"designed intuitive user experiences, developed user-centered data visualization systems, and implemented AI-powered tools to enhance productivity and user satisfaction."

Try

"Designed an AI sales workspace that cut support tickets 55% and reached 85% self-serve adoption. Won Dell's Bravo Innovation Award for it."

Why: generic competencies put you in the wrong category; specific, defensible nouns are what survive an interview. (Reuse the real numbers your home-page assistant already cites, so the whole site agrees.)

P1

Fix the hero headline so it says what you do, for whom

Now

"Curious by nature and a designer by choice, crafting interactions that bridge logic with emotion"

Try

"I design calm B2B software for the analysts and ops teams who run mortgage and lending workflows."

Why: "bridge logic with emotion" is generic portfolio poetry. Your own home page already nails the specific version ("B2B products and AI-powered workflows that keep financial teams moving"). Match it.

P1

Trade one principle block for one concrete story

You have four principles and five manifesto beliefs. Replace one with a short, specific story: a single decision on a single Tuesday, with the trade-off and the outcome. Behaviors land through a story; they evaporate as declarations.

P2

Elevate the AI-ownership card, and add a Loom

"I build with AI, and own the calls" is currently the last accordion item. Move it up; it is your single best 2026 signal. Then record a 5-minute Loom of you directing AI on a real screen, prompting and making judgment calls, and link it. The current guidance rates that above a polished case study.

P2

Move availability up, and drop one puffery word

"Open to senior IC roles in B2B & fintech" is buried in the footer. Put it near the top. And "30+ Transformative projects delivered" reads as inflation; cut "transformative" or make it concrete.
